Illuminations / Sacred Paper

Artist’s Statement

← Gallery

I make minutely hand-cut paper collages that read as illuminated works from books that do not exist—part sacred text, part guide for living. Working from diverse printed matter, I hold grief, desire, literature, and history in ceremony and study. Over six years I have composed 23 works—each complete in itself, yet together reading as an unbound manuscript for the education of “enlightened womanhood,” my revision of classical education. For instance, The Demon of History figures history as a desperate search for origin, while Conjugal Felicity and The Artist at Home center the practices of day-to-day life.

With rich borders, marginalia, and textual annotations, the collages clearly reference illuminated medieval manuscripts. They also show wisdom’s resistance to capture: paper—and the wisdoms inscribed on it—is always frail, assembled from scraps. Even so—precisely by foregrounding that frailty of paper—the works remake conditional truths into a more resilient kind of knowledge.

Motifs from a lifetime of humanistic study and craft recur to hold doubt in view. Threads and looms, as in Corps Féminine and Arachne, suggest the paradox between female experience and abstraction. Gates and doors, like those in The Ethiopian Gate and the Dream works, weigh safety against capture. By cutting apart the canon and re-stitching it, I remake conditional truths into working knowledge—works to be read, handled, and doubted.

Expanded Bio

Trudi Connolly is a Tucson-based visual artist working primarily in hand-cut paper collage. Born in Tucson, she has lived and studied in Sweden, France, China, New York, and California. She completed a BA/MA in Comparative Literature at New York University (French and critical theory), with additional studies in politics and sociology; she later taught English in China and pursued doctoral study in literature at the University of California, Irvine. Drawing on this humanistic and social-scientific training, Connolly assembles fragments of printed culture—manuscripts, marginalia, and vernacular ephemera—into manuscript-like works that examine history, domestic life, desire, and the fragile ways we hold knowledge. Cutting and re-stitching text and image, she composes speculative scriptures. A six-year cycle of 23 collages can be encountered individually or together, much like an unbound book.
